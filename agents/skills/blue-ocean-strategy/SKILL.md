# Blue Ocean Strategy

## Domain Context

Most companies compete in **red oceans** — known market space, known rules, competitors
fighting over the same demand, differentiation and low cost treated as a trade-off.
**Blue oceans** are uncontested market space created by breaking that trade-off: pursuing
differentiation *and* low cost simultaneously through **value innovation**.

Value innovation isn't about beating the competition on their terms (better features,
lower price on the existing offering) — it's about making the competition irrelevant by
reconstructing what the industry competes on in the first place.

## When to Use

- The business is stuck in head-to-head competition on the industry's existing factors
- Looking for a breakthrough repositioning, not an incremental improvement
- Deciding what to strip out of an offering as much as what to add
- Triggers: blue ocean, red ocean, value innovation, uncontested market, ERRC grid,
  four actions framework

## The Four Actions Framework

Challenge the industry's competing factors with four disciplined questions:

1. **Eliminate** — which factors that the industry takes for granted should be
   eliminated entirely?
2. **Reduce** — which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard?
3. **Raise** — which factors should be raised well above the industry standard?
4. **Create** — which factors should be created that the industry has never offered?

**Eliminate + Reduce** drive the cost side down. **Raise + Create** drive the value side
up. Both happen together — that's what breaks the differentiation/cost trade-off.

## The ERRC Grid

Lay the four actions out as a 2x2 grid against the industry's current competing factors
(price, features, service level, distribution, whatever the industry actually competes
on today):

```
  ELIMINATE                 |  RAISE
  [factors to drop]         |  [factors to push above standard]
  --------------------------+--------------------------
  REDUCE                    |  CREATE
  [factors to cut back]     |  [factors that don't exist yet]
```

The result is a new value curve — a strategic profile that looks different from every
competitor's, not just better on the same axes.

## Prompt

You are a strategy advisor applying Blue Ocean Strategy to $ARGUMENTS.

**Step 1: Map the industry's current competing factors** — what does everyone in this
space compete on today (price, feature X, service level, speed, brand, etc.)?

**Step 2: Build the ERRC grid** — for each competing factor, and any new ones, sort into
Eliminate / Reduce / Raise / Create. Push for at least one real entry in Create — if
nothing is genuinely new, this is optimization, not blue ocean strategy.

**Step 3: Sketch the new value curve** — plot the current industry's strategic profile
against the proposed one across the competing factors; the proposed curve should look
distinctly different, not just "better everywhere."

**Step 4: Check the cost/value logic** — does Eliminate+Reduce genuinely lower cost
structure, and does Raise+Create genuinely create new buyer value? If the grid only moves
factors up (all "raise", nothing eliminated), this is expensive differentiation, not value
innovation.

**Step 5: Identify who the "non-customers" are** — who currently rejects, refuses, or
doesn't consider this industry's offerings at all, and would this new positioning bring
them in?

## Output Process

1. List today's industry competing factors
2. Fill the ERRC grid
3. Draw/describe the resulting value curve vs. the industry's current curve
4. Validate the cost/value logic (both sides move, not just one)
5. Identify the non-customer segment this move could unlock

## Notes

- A real blue ocean move usually feels uncomfortable to a founder trained on the
  industry's existing rules — if every stakeholder immediately agrees, the ERRC grid
  probably isn't aggressive enough.
- This complements `porters-five-forces` (which analyzes competing *within* the current
  industry structure) — blue ocean strategy asks whether to opt out of that structure
  instead.
