# Value Chain Analysis

## Domain Context

Introduced by Michael Porter (*Competitive Advantage*, 1985), the value chain breaks a
business into the discrete activities it performs to design, produce, market, deliver, and
support its product — then asks where each activity adds cost and where it adds value.
Competitive advantage comes from doing these activities better or differently than
competitors, not from the business as an undifferentiated whole.

## When to Use

- Looking for cost-reduction opportunities without a clear place to start
- Looking for differentiation opportunities beyond the product itself
- Trying to understand where a competitor's actual advantage comes from
- Triggers: value chain, Porter value chain, primary and support activities, where does
  our value come from

## Primary Activities

Directly create and deliver the product:

1. **Inbound Logistics** — receiving, storing, and distributing inputs
2. **Operations** — transforming inputs into the final product/service
3. **Outbound Logistics** — getting the finished product to customers
4. **Marketing & Sales** — getting customers to buy
5. **Service** — supporting customers after the sale

## Support Activities

Enable the primary activities to function:

1. **Firm Infrastructure** — management, finance, legal, quality control
2. **Human Resource Management** — recruiting, training, retention
3. **Technology Development** — R&D, product/process innovation, tooling
4. **Procurement** — purchasing inputs, negotiating with suppliers

## Prompt

You are a strategy advisor running a value chain analysis for $ARGUMENTS.

**Step 1: Map every activity** the business performs into the nine boxes (5 primary + 4
support) — be specific to this business, not generic.

**Step 2: Identify cost drivers per activity** — where is money actually being spent, and
is that spend proportional to the value that activity creates?

**Step 3: Identify differentiation sources per activity** — which activities, if
strengthened, would make customers value the product more (not just cost more)?

**Step 4: Find the linkages** — where does one activity's cost/quality depend on another
(e.g. better Procurement lowering Operations cost, or better Technology Development
speeding up Operations)? Linkages are often where the real advantage or waste hides, not
any single activity in isolation.

**Step 5: Compare against competitors** where possible — which activities does this
business perform better, worse, or fundamentally differently?

## Output Process

1. Map all nine activity boxes for the business
2. Tag each with cost driver and/or differentiation potential
3. Identify cross-activity linkages
4. Flag 2-3 activities as the highest-leverage targets for improvement
5. Note any activity that should be outsourced (low differentiation potential, not a core
   advantage) vs. kept in-house (source of real advantage)

## Notes

- Not every activity needs to be world-class — the point is to find which ones actually
  drive cost or differentiation, and focus there, rather than optimizing everything
  equally.
- Pairs well with `porters-five-forces` (industry-level competitive structure) and
  `blue-ocean-strategy` (which activities to eliminate/reduce/raise/create).
