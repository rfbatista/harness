# Investor Updates

## Domain Context

Startups that send consistent investor updates are roughly twice as likely to raise
follow-on funding compared to those that go quiet between rounds. Regular updates build
trust, signal control, and keep investors confident and ready to help.

A good update is supposed to be **boring** — it looks the same month after month, in
structure and tone. That sameness is what builds trust over time: investors can scan it in
under a minute and know exactly where to look for the number that matters.

## When to Use

- Writing a monthly or milestone investor update
- Setting up a recurring update cadence from scratch
- Reviewing a draft update before sending
- Triggers: investor update, board update, monthly update, shareholder update

## The Six-Block Structure

1. **Subject line with an anchor metric** — e.g. "Acme, May 2026: MRR $47k, up 18%", not
   "Monthly Update"
2. **TL;DR** — two or three sentences, state of the union, readable without opening
   anything else
3. **Metrics** — the numbers, with month-over-month change, not just the raw figure
4. **Wins** — what actually moved the business forward this period
5. **Lowlights / what's not working** — stated plainly, no spin; this is where trust is
   won or lost
6. **Asks** — specific help needed: an intro, a hire, a customer referral, feedback on a
   decision

## Metrics to Include

- **MRR/ARR** (or the revenue-equivalent for the stage)
- **Cash in the bank**
- **Monthly burn**
- **Runway in months** (see `cap-table-and-runway` for the calculation)
- **One or two model-specific metrics** — DAU, conversion rate, gross margin, whatever the
  North Star for this business actually is (see `north-star-metric`)

Always show the number **and** the change from last period — the trend matters more than
the raw figure.

## Length and Cadence

- **500-750 words total.** Past ~1,000 words, engagement falls off sharply — most
  investors read on their phone between meetings.
- **Monthly** is the right cadence for early-stage startups: frequent enough to keep
  investors informed, and a forcing function to actually review your own numbers.
- Start fundraising conversations with ~6 months of runway left, not when it's urgent —
  the update is the channel that keeps that conversation warm the whole time.

## Prompt

You are helping a founder draft an investor update for $ARGUMENTS.

**Step 1: Write the subject line** — pick the one anchor metric that best summarizes the
period, with its direction of change.

**Step 2: Draft the TL;DR** — 2-3 sentences, no jargon, readable in 10 seconds.

**Step 3: List metrics with month-over-month deltas** — MRR/ARR, cash, burn, runway, plus
1-2 model-specific numbers.

**Step 4: Write wins and lowlights separately** — lowlights must be as plainly stated as
wins. No burying bad news in qualifiers.

**Step 5: Write specific asks** — not "let us know if you can help," but named asks: an
intro to a specific type of person/company, feedback on a specific decision, help filling
a specific role.

**Step 6: Cut to 500-750 words** — if it's longer, move detail to a linked doc rather than
padding the update itself.

## Output Process

1. Confirm the anchor metric for the subject line
2. Draft each of the six blocks in order
3. Verify every metric has a stated delta, not just a raw number
4. Verify at least one lowlight is present if anything is actually not working — an update
   with no lowlights reads as unreliable, not as good news
5. Confirm asks are specific and actionable
6. Check total length against the 500-750 word target

## Notes

- Consistency of structure across months is itself the signal — don't redesign the format
  each time.
- If a metric would look bad this month, that's exactly the metric to include, with
  context on why and what's being done about it.
