# Hiring the Founding Team

## Domain Context

Your first 10 hires define your company's DNA. The founding engineer slot is the most
critical of all — they decide what gets built, how fast it ships, and what technical debt
the company lives with for years. A strong first engineer turns incomplete ideas into
working software, spots risks the founder can't see, establishes sensible technical
practices, and helps recruit the people who follow them.

Most successful early teams prioritize **senior** engineers for the first 3-5 hires,
because they set the technical patterns and architectural foundations every later engineer
inherits. Junior hires early mean the founding engineer (or founder) is doing that
foundational work alone anyway, just slower.

## When to Use

- Writing a job description for an early hire
- Designing an interview process for a founding engineer or first employee
- Deciding who to hire first, and in what order
- Triggers: first engineer, founding engineer, first hire, early-stage hiring, interview
  process for startup

## Finding Candidates

**Top engineers don't apply — go to them.** In priority order:
1. **Referrals** — from your own network, investors, advisors; highest signal, fastest close
2. **Outbound sourcing** — lead with the problem you're solving, not the job title; make
   the equity tangible (real numbers, not just a percentage)
3. **Hiring platforms** — widest reach, lowest signal; expect more filtering work

A job description should set clear expectations (role, responsibilities, qualifications,
comp/equity range) and also sell the company: culture, why now, what makes this worth the
risk relative to a stable job elsewhere.

## Designing the Interview Loop

- Prefer **multi-part questions** that reveal how someone thinks, over single
  gotcha/trivia questions
- Avoid overly difficult or "trick" questions — look for thoughtful, knowledgeable answers
  with good follow-ups, not a pass/fail puzzle
- Consider **contract-to-hire** for early roles: it's a real way to evaluate someone on
  actual work, and lets them evaluate the startup back, before either side commits fully
- Keep the loop short — early-stage candidates are usually interviewing elsewhere too, and
  a slow process loses the best ones

## Prompt

You are a startup hiring advisor helping a founder hire for $ARGUMENTS.

**Step 1: Confirm this is the right hire, in the right order** — is this one of the first
3-5 technical hires (bias toward senior) or a later, more specialized role (junior is
fine)?

**Step 2: Draft the job description** — role, responsibilities, qualifications, comp/equity
range, and a section that sells the company (why now, what's the bet).

**Step 3: Pick the sourcing channel(s)** — referrals first, then targeted outbound, then
platforms; don't default to job boards alone for a founding hire.

**Step 4: Design the interview loop** — 2-4 rounds max for an early hire; include at least
one multi-part, think-out-loud problem; avoid trivia/gotcha questions; consider whether a
contract-to-hire trial fits this role.

**Step 5: Define what "yes" looks like** — write down the bar before interviewing anyone,
so the process doesn't drift based on who's in the room that week.

## Output Process

1. Confirm hire priority and seniority target
2. Draft or review the job description
3. Choose sourcing channels in priority order
4. Design the interview loop (rounds, question types, decision criteria)
5. Decide if a contract-to-hire trial period fits
6. Set the equity/comp range up front, tied to the cap table (see `cap-table-and-runway`)

## Notes

- Speed matters more early: the best early-stage candidates have options and won't wait
  through a slow, multi-week process.
- Equity that's "tangible" means showing what it could be worth in a real scenario, not
  just quoting a percentage with no context.
- A bad early hire is expensive twice: once in runway spent, once in the patterns and
  culture that later hires inherit from them.
